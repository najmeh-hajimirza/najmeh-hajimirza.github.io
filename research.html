Correlated De-Risking: How Carbon Pricing Reshapes the Structure and Leakage of Bank Lending
Working paper · 2025

Carbon-pricing policies are designed to reduce fossil exposure in the financial system, but less is known about how they reshape bank portfolios and whether they shift carbon-intensive credit across borders. This paper studies 25 carbon-pricing events between 2005 and 2023 and measures banks' treatment intensity using their pre-policy exposure to affected borrowers. I examine how syndicated lenders adjust their portfolios after borrower countries introduce carbon taxes or emissions-trading systems. Three results emerge. First, more exposed banks reduce their brown-core lending share by about half a percentage point per standard deviation of pre-policy exposure. Second, the fossil portfolios of exposed banks become more similar in their sub-sector composition, suggesting that de-risking is correlated across institutions. Third, within the same borrower and quarter, treated banks increase lending to brown-core borrowers in untreated countries by roughly 20–25 percent. These findings show that carbon pricing reduces the scale of fossil lending, but also changes its structure and location. The paper highlights two policy-relevant channels: correlated bank de-risking and cross-border leakage in fossil credit.

Biodiversity Risks and Lender Credit Allocation: The Role of Lenders' Commitments to Environmental Sustainability
With Hossein Asgharian, Sara Jonsson, and Lu Liu · SSRN · November 2025

This paper investigates how financial institutions incorporate biodiversity risks into lending and loan resale decisions, comparing UNEP FI–committed and non-committed institutions. Using global loan-level data from Reuters DealScan (2010–2023), we analyze how lenders adjust loan size and secondary-market sales in response to firms' biodiversity impact and dependency risks. We find that all lenders treat biodiversity dependency as a financially material risk, extending smaller loans to more biodiversity-dependent firms. By contrast, only committed lenders respond to impact risk by reducing credit to high-impact borrowers and, when industry differences are not controlled for, appear more likely to offload such exposures in the secondary market. This suggests that resale behavior mainly reflects sectoral variation in biodiversity intensity rather than firm-level risk. Overall, non-committed lenders focus on financial materiality, while committed lenders internalize double materiality by aligning credit allocation and portfolio management with sustainability objectives.
